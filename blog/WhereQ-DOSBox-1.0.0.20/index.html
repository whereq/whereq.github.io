WhereQ-DOSBox 1.0.0.20: the save button on the gamepad

November 27, 2026 · 8 min read
Dazhi Zhang (Tony)

The moment you did not want to lose​

Twenty minutes into Lode Runner, on the fifth floor, with the ladder you just carried up the wall. The kind of state you would like to be able to come back to.

And then the thought: save this.

But the gamepad cannot save.

Game Mode is the mode where the app stops being a keyboard and becomes a console — an eight-way D-pad under the left thumb, face buttons under the right, the DOS picture in the middle. It is the mode you play in. And saving a state lives on the far side of the app, in the launcher, in the keyboard mode. To save, you had to type the reverse Konami code on the gamepad's own D-pad (↑ ↑ ↓ ↓ ← → ← →, then X, Y), land back in the keyboard, open the launcher, find the game, open States, and pick a slot.

The game kept running the whole time. That is the part that is dangerous: you can save after the thing you wanted to save has already happened.

So you stopped playing. Or you played on without a safety net. Or you did the four-gesture dance and hoped you would still remember what you were doing when you came back.

The report that asked for this release was three sentences long, and all three of them were about saving: the gamepad had no way to save, three slots was too few, and two saves from "yesterday" both read "yesterday", with no way to tell which one was newer.

This release answers all three.

A green button that saves the machine​

The gamepad's top bar now carries a SAVE chip, between the gold profile chip and ESC. It is green, because green is what a save looks like everywhere else in the app.

Tap it and the running machine is frozen straight away, into the first free slot. When all five slots are full, it goes into the oldest one — so the four newest saves are the ones that survive, and the oldest is the one you agreed to lose.

Hold it and the Save states sheet opens over the gamepad: five slots, the game still running behind them, the gamepad still there when the sheet closes. The launcher that normally sits underneath is kept out of the way, so what you see is a sheet of the game, not a dialog of the launcher.

And a small toast says which slot took the save — Saved to slot 2 — because the launcher line that usually carries that message is not on screen in Game Mode. A save that happens without a word is a save you cannot trust.

The green SAVE chip in the top bar, between the gold profile chip and ESC.

Hold the SAVE chip and the sheet opens over the gamepad, with the game still running behind it.

Five slots, one number​

Three slots was not enough. The count is now one constant, SaveStore.STATE_SLOTS, read by the sheet, by the "newest slot" search and by the durable backup alike, so no two parts of the app can disagree about how many slots there are.

Slots 4 and 5 are new files. The three older slots keep their numbers and their contents, which is the point of the constant: nothing had to move to make room.

Two saves from the same day​

A save made today reads just now, 12 minutes ago or 3 hours ago.

A save made yesterday used to read yesterday. Two saves from yesterday therefore read the same, and the sheet could not tell you which was newer — a coin flip between two states, with no way to reason about it.

From a previous day on, the sheet carries the clock time as well: Yesterday 21:40 for yesterday, and Nov 25 09:12 for anything older. Two saves from the same day can now be put in order by eye.

Slot 1 · 12 minutes ago, and two saves made yesterday that read Yesterday 10:00 and Yesterday 20:00.

Your save states still load​

The save-state format did not change. Every save state written by the earlier releases loads normally.

Nothing in this release touches the emulator's internals — it is an app-layer release, like the ones before it — but this is the sentence that has to be in every release note, so here it is again.

The guide grew to match​

The user guide now shows the SAVE button in the top bar, the sheet floating over the gamepad, five slots in it, and a slot that carries its clock time.

Every frame was re-taken from the release-signed 1.0.0.20 APK — the artifact people actually download, not a local build — so the pictures in the guide, in the repository and on the PC Legends site are the same ones. The guide that walked through every screen of the app now walks through the new button too, in English and in Chinese.

Get it​

The release is on the releases page as tag v1.0.0.20. The APK is published as a release asset built by CI from that tag, with a .sha256 sidecar next to it, so you can check what you downloaded.

👉 github.com/whereq/whereq-dosbox — the source, and the whole history of it.

It runs on arm64-v8a, armeabi-v7a and x86_64 devices, Android 6.0 and newer, entirely offline, with no account and no tracking. The project is GPL-2.0, and the DOSBox core is embedded with its AUTHORS, ChangeLog, COPYING and per-file headers intact.

If you are new here, the announcement says what the project is, and the user guide walks through every screen of it. The console that the keyboard became is about the pads Game Mode replaces, the save that outlives the app about the slots that still load, the gamepad for your thumb about the second face of the keyboard, and the keyboard you already own about the Bluetooth one. For the whole picture of the project, read WhereQ-DOSBox; for what a key does inside the machine, the interrupt map has it.

Again, this post is not the documentation. The documentation is the repository: the user guide, the technical reference, and the release note. Please read those rather than take my word for it.

Thank you, DOSBox​

None of this is possible without the DOSBox project.

DOSBox was written by the DOSBox Team — a volunteer crew who took an operating system from 1994 and made it runnable on every machine the world has produced since, including the ones that did not exist yet. They kept it alive for three decades and gave it away to anyone who wanted it. WhereQ-DOSBox is a thin layer on top of their work, and this release is a thin layer on top of that. The originality, the craft and the generosity are entirely theirs.

This port is built on Em-DOSBox, the Emscripten build of DOSBox r4305 by Boris Gjenero, which grew out of Ismail Khatib's first attempt to compile DOSBox with Emscripten, and rode on the Emscripten work of Alon Zakai. The SDL2 library by Sam Lantinga carries the window, the audio and the touch input — and the hardware keyboard too.

DOSBox is a trademark of the DOSBox project; this port is not affiliated with or endorsed by the DOSBox team.

A console that cannot save is a console you play carefully. Now it can save.
