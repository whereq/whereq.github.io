WhereQ-DOSBox 1.0.0.9: the keyboard becomes a handheld console

November 16, 2026 · 10 min read
Dazhi Zhang (Tony)

A DOS game asks for a keyboard. The phone does not have one.

Until this release the answer was two thin columns of keys standing in the black bars beside the DOS picture: the modifiers on one side, File, Ent, Space and the arrow keys on the other.

Two columns, one key each, the DOS picture in the middle. It typed. It was also nothing like the thing it was standing in for.

It typed. You could type a path, move a cursor, hold a key down while something else happened on the screen. It worked.

What it did not do was anything a thumb could do. A DOS game is played with the left thumb on the arrows and the right thumb on the keys, and every one of those keys was a long way from where a thumb expects to find it. The picture was 4:3, the phone was 20:9, and the difference between them was exactly the space the keys were trying to live in.

What a handheld console does​

A handheld console puts the picture in the middle and the controls in your hands — one hand on each side — and it never puts a control on top of the picture.

That is the whole idea behind 1.0.0.9. The DOS picture keeps the middle of the screen, and the keys move out into the two black bars beside it and take the shape of two pads.

The left pad carries the arrows and the left half of the keyboard, the right pad the action keys and the right half. The picture keeps the middle, and nothing is drawn over it.

The left pad is the thumb that steers. The four arrow keys are a D-pad — up on top, left and right side by side, down below, so the finger reads the direction by shape alone — and above them sits the left half of a split QWERTY, with File, Esc and Tab along the top.

The right pad is the thumb that acts. Alt, Ctrl, Shift, Space and Enter under the right thumb, the right half of the QWERTY above them, and #+=, Fn and Backspace along the top.

The QWERTY is split rather than shrunk. 12345 qwert asdfg zxcvb on the left, 67890 yuiop hjkl: nm./\ on the right — each half is a real key size, not a miniature of the whole thing squeezed into a corner of the screen.

Three layers, one key each​

A split keyboard only solves half the problem; the other half is what you do when you run out of keys. The answer here is one key per layer, in the top row of the right pad.

#+= swaps both halves for the punctuation. The key lights up blue while its layer is on, and turns into abc, because that is what pressing it brings you back to.

The punctuation layer. Every shifted character sits directly below its unshifted key, and the switched key is lit.

Fn brings up the functions: F1 to F12 on the left, and Ins, Del, Home, End, PgUp, PgDn, Caps, PrtSc and Pause on the right.

The function layer — the whole F-row and the editing keys, without stealing a single key from the letters.

And because the letter layer of the right half already carries : . / \, typing a DOS path needs no layer switch at all: C:\GAMES\DUNE\SETUP.EXE is letters and punctuation, in that order, on one layer.

Ctrl, Alt and Shift, the way a game wants them​

The modifiers are the part I thought about longest, because two different games want two different things from Ctrl.

A tap on Ctrl, Alt or Shift arms it: the next key you press is modified, and the modifier releases itself. Ctrl, then C — that is what a text editor or a DOS shell wants.

A hold keeps it pressed for as long as your finger is down, like a real key. That is what a game wants from its fire and strafe keys: hold Ctrl on the right thumb while the left thumb walks the D-pad, and the game sees both at once.

The old keyboard could only do the first one. The new pads do both, because a modifier is nothing more than a key that is down.

The layout follows the screen​

Not every phone has 20:9 of letterbox to work with, so the keyboard picks its shape from the width of the bar:

the letterbox barwhat the keyboard does
at least 116 dpthe full pads, on both sides
narrower than thatone key column per side, and the whole QWERTY folds up from the bottom
portraita slim strip of keys along the bottom, with the same fold-up QWERTY

Here is the part I am not happy about.

The same layout on an 18:9 screen. The pads still fit, but the letters are small — about 24 dp wide, against 33 dp on a 20:9 screen.

The key sizes, measured on the emulator at 420 dpi:

screenbarletter keykey height
20:9 (2400×1080)183 dp32.8 dp wide44 dp
18:9 (2160×1080)137 dp23.6 dp wide44 dp

An 18:9 bar is 137 dp and the pads need 116 dp, so nothing falls back to the single columns — but the letters get squeezed. 23.6 dp is about 2.9 % of the screen width, where a normal phone keyboard key is roughly twice that. The D-pad and the action keys keep their full size; it is the letters that get tight.

I measured it rather than guessed: both widths were run on the emulator and typed into, and the screenshots of all three layers at both widths are in the repository. What I have not done is try it on a physical 18:9 phone, and that is the gap I would want a reader with such a phone to close for me.

Nothing underneath changed​

The whole release is app-layer work. The SDL surface stays full-screen, the keyboard only ever draws in the bars beside the picture, and libdosbox.so is the same library 1.0.0.8 shipped — the same native core, compiled with the same flags.

Sometimes the honest way to describe a big change is to say what did not change.

Get it​

The release APK is on the releases page, signed with the release key, so it installs over 1.0.0.8 in place:

👉 github.com/whereq/whereq-dosbox — tag v1.0.0.9

whereq-dosbox-1.0.0.9.apk
size:   15.7 MB
sha256: cd6d0bdafd0f84c7324936c89a6cab14ed87a21e4211ed8909078b4a7a51ac02

It runs on arm64-v8a, armeabi-v7a and x86_64 devices, Android 5.0 and newer, entirely offline, with no account and no tracking. The project is GPL-2.0, and the DOSBox core is embedded with its AUTHORS, ChangeLog, COPYING and per-file headers intact.

The repository is the documentation​

This post is not the manual. The manual is the repository: README says what the app is, docs/TECHNICAL-REFERENCE.md is the whole implementation in one document — including the section that explains the three width rules and the measured key sizes — docs/USER-GUIDE.md is the guide for the person holding the phone, and docs/INTERRUPTS.md is the interrupt map. Please read those rather than take my word for it.

The rest of the story is here: DOSBox inside an APK, the whole machine in one file, the save that outlives the app, a console-style gamepad for your thumb. The announcement is where this started, and the project today is where it stands.

If you have a phone with a narrow screen and the letters feel too small — tell me. That is the one thing in this release I would like to have seen on real hardware before shipping it, and I would rather hear it from you than find it out myself.

Thank you, DOSBox​

None of this is possible without the DOSBox project.

DOSBox was written by the DOSBox Team — a volunteer crew who took an operating system from 1994 and made it runnable on every machine the world has produced since, including the ones that did not exist yet. They kept it alive for three decades and gave it away to anyone who wanted it. WhereQ-DOSBox is a thin layer on top of their work. The originality, the craft and the generosity are entirely theirs.

This port is built on Em-DOSBox, the Emscripten build of DOSBox r4305 by Boris Gjenero, which grew out of Ismail Khatib's first attempt to compile DOSBox with Emscripten, and rode on the Emscripten work of Alon Zakai. The SDL2 library by Sam Lantinga carries the window, the audio and the touch input.
