WhereQ-DOSBox user guide: put a DOS game on your phone in five minutes

2026年11月18日 · 阅读需 24 分钟
Dazhi Zhang (Tony)

The guide I wanted and could not find​

Here is a thing I noticed while I was testing the app on my own phone. Every DOSBox guide I have ever read was written by somebody who already knew what a .zip bundle was. They start at the interesting part — the sound chips, the save-state format, the JIT — and assume you have been running DOS since 1994.

That assumption is wrong for most people holding a phone. The hardest part of putting an old game on a modern phone is not the emulator. It is the first five minutes: getting the game onto the phone at all, in a shape the app can start.

So I wrote the guide I wanted instead. Seven steps, one screenshot of every screen, and no assumption that you have ever seen a command prompt.

The five-minute version​

  1. Install the app. The APK is on the releases page.
  2. Open it. You land on the Files panel, and it is empty.
  3. Press + Install a bundle (.zip) and pick a .zip game file.
  4. Press Play. The game starts.
  5. Press File (top left) whenever you want to come back to the list.

That is the whole thing. Everything below is the detail behind those five steps — read it if you want to understand what you are doing, skip it if you just want to play.

Before you start​

PhoneAndroid 5.0 or newer, any phone or tablet
Spaceabout 60 MB for the app, plus the size of the game
Networknone needed once the app and the game are on the phone
Accountnone

You also need a game, as a single .zip file. A game for this app is not the same thing as a ZIP of your own files — see Where do I get a game? below.

Step 1 — Install the app​

The app is not in the source repository. It is built by the release pipeline and published on the releases page as whereq-dosbox-<version>.apk, with a .sha256 file next to it carrying the checksum.

Copy the .apk to your phone — cable, cloud drive, a file-sharing app, whatever is at hand — then tap it on the phone. Android asks whether to allow installing from that app; allow it, and the install goes through.

The app appears on your home screen as the >_ mark:

The WhereQ-DOSBox icon on the home screen.

The name in the app list, the notification, and the screen Android shows for the app all read WhereQ-DOSBox:

The Android App info page for WhereQ-DOSBox.

Step 2 — Install a game​

Open the app. You land in the Files panel — the screen that lists what you have installed:

The Files panel on a fresh install, with no games yet.

Two ways to get a game in, both at the top of the panel:

buttonwhat it does
+ Install a bundle (.zip)pick one .zip from your phone
Install all bundles from a folderunpack every .zip in one folder at once

Press + Install a bundle (.zip), and the system's file picker opens. Pick your game's .zip — usually in Downloads — and the app unpacks it and registers it. The panel then lists it:

The Files panel with two games installed.

Each game in the list has four buttons:

buttonwhat it does
Playstart the game from the beginning
Statessave and load save states for this game
Launch…run a different program, or add driver files, before the game
Removeuninstall this game from the app

Where do I get a game?​

A game for this app is a .zip with a small, fixed shape:

  the bundle
  ─────────
  PLAY.BAT          the entry point - the app runs this
  <the game files>  whatever the game needs
  __meta.json       the name the app shows in the list

That is it. The repository ships one, so you can see the shape and try the app immediately: examples/game-bundle — the STARFALL demo. Copy whereq-demo-bundle.zip from that folder to your phone and install it.

For real games: sites that still host abandonware DOS games generally offer them as a .zip. If a game's archive contains the game files but no PLAY.BAT, the app cannot start it — make a PLAY.BAT that runs the game's executable, and zip the folder again.

Step 3 — Play the game​

Press Play.

The emulator restarts the app and boots the game, and you land at a DOS prompt:

The DOSBox welcome banner and the C:\> prompt, with the two keyboard pads beside the picture.

Type the game's name and press Enter. From then on it is the real game — the real keyboard, the real sound, the real mouse.

Here is a real 1994 DOS game running on a phone:

Formula 1 (Domark, 1994) running on the phone, with the on-screen keyboard around it.

And here is the STARFALL demo:

The STARFALL title screen.

STARFALL being played, with the two keyboard pads beside the picture.

The first time a game runs, Android may ask whether the app may send you notifications. It uses them for a "back to the game" reminder when you leave the app. Answer either way — declining only means you lose that reminder:

The Android notification permission prompt.

When a game is running, the File key (top left) brings the Files panel back over it, and the panel shows a Resume line for the game that is running right now:

The Files panel over a running game, with Resume F1 above the list.

Step 4 — The keyboard and the mouse​

There is no physical keyboard in the way, because the screen keyboard is the keyboard. In landscape you get two pads beside the picture, the way a handheld console is built: the left pad has the arrow keys as a D-pad and the left half of a split QWERTY, the right pad has the action keys (Alt, Ctrl, Shift, Space, Enter) and the right half of the alphabet.

Two keys change what the pads show:

keyshows
#+=the punctuation layer
FnF1–F12, Ins/Del, Home/End, PgUp/PgDn, Caps, PrtSc, Pause

The switched key lights up while its layer is on.

The #+= layer — the punctuation sits where the letters were.

The Fn layer — the whole F-row and the editing keys, without stealing a single key from the letters.

Modifiers work two ways. Tap Ctrl, Alt or Shift and it stays armed for the next key — Ctrl, then C. Hold it and it behaves like a real key for as long as your finger is down, which is what a game expects of its fire and strafe keys while the other thumb steers.

The mouse is the screen. Your finger is the mouse: drag to move the pointer, tap to click, and the on-screen RMB key (on the left pad, next to Tab) is the right mouse button — in most DOS games that is cancel or back. A second finger on the picture does the same, at that finger's own position.

Turn the phone to portrait and the pads fold into a single strip along the bottom, for the games that are taller than they are wide.

Game Mode — a gamepad for action games​

Some games want a gamepad, not a typewriter. Game Mode replaces the keyboard pads with a console-style controller: a direction pad and a trackball, with the buttons a DOS game actually fires, jumps and strafes with.

Game Mode. The letter pads are gone; a D-pad and a trackball take their place, in the same two bars.

Getting in and out is a nod to the old days. With the keyboard pads showing, switch Caps Lock on first — double-tap Shift, and the key turns blue and reads CAPS — then enter the Konami code on the pads — ↑ ↑ ↓ ↓ ← → ← → B A — and the pads flip into Game Mode. The B and A must be capital: the code watches the letters in the case you actually type them, so a lowercase "ba" just types "ba" into the DOS prompt. To go back, enter it again on the gamepad: the ↑ ↑ ↓ ↓ ← → ← → on the D-pad, then X, Y (the two face buttons stand in for B and A). The phone's Back button also returns you to the keyboard.

The gold button at the top-left names the current profile; tap it to cycle, long-press for a picker. Each game remembers the profile and D-pad you last used with it.

Long-press the gold profile button and the picker opens — the three profiles, and the way into the remap screen.

profileleft thumbright thumbfor
Hybrid (default)8-way D-padtrackballgames that mix keys and mouse — Dune II, X-COM, Doom with a mouse
Action8-way D-padFIRE / JUMP / USE / ENT buttonskeyboard arcade/shooters — Wolf3D, Keen, Prince of Persia
Pointbig LEFT / RIGHT mouse buttonstrackballmouse-only games — Monkey Island, Civilization, Lemmings

The Action profile: FIRE, JUMP, USE and ENT, for the games that fire and jump on the keyboard.

The Point & click profile: the mouse buttons take the whole left pad, the trackball the right one.

The trackball moves the pointer the way a laptop touchpad does — stroke to move, tap to click — rather than jumping it to where you touch. In Hybrid, holding L-CLICK (left shoulder) while you roll the trackball is a drag-select.

The D-pad output — ARROWS, NUMPAD, WASD​

DOS games don't agree on which keys "up" is. The ARROWS button under the D-pad cycles what the pad sends, and the app remembers your choice per game:

modeup / down / left / righta diagonal (e.g. up-right)choose it for
ARROWSthe arrow keys ↑ ↓ ← →two keys at once (↑ + →)most games — anything that reads the cursor keys
NUMPADthe numeric keypad 8 / 2 / 4 / 6a single key — 7 / 9 / 1 / 3games built for the numpad, where a diagonal is its own key (many roguelikes and joystick-via-keypad games)
WASDthe letters W / A / S / Dtwo keys at once (W + D)later shooters and anything mapped to WASD

So the difference is both which keys the pad presses and how a diagonal is sent: ARROWS and WASD press the two neighbouring keys together, while NUMPAD has a real key for each of the eight directions. If your game ignores the D-pad, it is almost always the wrong mode — switch with the ARROWS button until it responds.

Remapping a button​

Sometimes a game wants a key the pad does not have — F to fire, SPACE to jump, the numeric keypad to shoot. You can teach any Game Mode button to send a different key, and the app remembers it for that game.

Long-press the gold profile button (top-left, the one that names the profile) and choose Remap buttons…. The screen enters remap mode: a banner along the top says "Tap a button to remap", and the buttons you can change are outlined in gold. Tap one and a list of keys opens — pick the one you want. DONE (or the banner) leaves remap mode.

Remap mode: the gold outline marks the buttons you can change; on the gold FIRE button it has to be dark brown, or it would disappear.

you can changeyou cannot change
CTRL, ALT, SHIFT, TAB, STR ◂, STR ▸, SPACE, ENT, USE, JUMP and FIREthe X and Y buttons (they answer the game's Quit? (Y/N) prompts and the Konami code), ESC, the D-pad, the trackball, the mouse buttons, and the function chips (ARROWS, Fn, 1–9, TURBO, SPEED, DRAG, DBL)

A changed button shows the new key instead of its old name — FIRE keeps its name and shows the key under it. Reset to default, the first entry in the list, puts the button back.

Remaps are per game and cover every profile, so a game remembers what each button sends whichever profile you play it in.

Step 5 — Save a state​

A save state is the whole machine frozen: the CPU, the memory, the video memory, the sound chips, the open DOS file handles. Load it and the game is exactly where you left it — even if the app was closed in between.

Press File, then States next to the game:

The Save states sheet, with three empty slots.

There are three slots. Press Save to freeze the game into slot 1, and the sheet shows when you did it:

The sheet with a saved slot — Slot 1, two minutes ago.

Close the sheet and the Files panel carries a Resume line for the game, naming the slot and the moment it was made:

The Files panel with Resume starfall-v2 · slot 1 · just now.

Load puts the game back. A slot survives quitting the app and rebooting the phone.

Step 6 — Keep your saves safe​

A save state normally lives inside the app's own private storage. Android deletes that when you uninstall the app — and when you lose the phone, or switch phones. Your games and your saves go with it.

So the app asks, once, whether you want a second copy somewhere that is yours:

The Keep your saves safe prompt, after the first save.

The app says the same thing in its own dialog, which you can open at any time:

The Saves folder dialog, with no folder set yet.

Press Choose folder, and the system asks you to allow the app into a folder on your phone:

The system permission dialog for the folder.

Pick a folder, press USE THIS FOLDER, and press ALLOW. From then on every slot is mirrored into that folder, compressed, next to the game itself.

You can see, and change, the folder at any time under Config → Saves folder:

The Settings dialog.

The Saves folder dialog, with a folder set.

Two notes:

  • Android refuses to let an app take the Download folder itself, because that would be too broad a permission. Pick any other folder — Documents is fine.
  • You only have to answer this once. The app does not ask again.

Step 7 — After a reinstall, or on a new phone​

This is what the folder is for.

Uninstall the app, install it again, and choose the same folder. The app looks inside it and finds your games — they are still there, because they were never inside the app:

The folder on the phone after the app was uninstalled.

The app notices the games that are not installed and offers to bring them back:

The Restore games prompt.

Press Restore, and they come back with their names and their saves:

Restored two games, with their saves ready.

And the save state still knows when it was made:

The restored slot, still saying three minutes ago.

That last line is the point of the whole feature. A save that was made three minutes ago says three minutes ago, even after it was rebuilt from the folder on a fresh install.

If something goes wrong​

what you seewhat it meanswhat to do
"That save file is damaged"the slot was written by an older versionthe save format grew a version; older slots are refused rather than misread. Start a new slot.
The game does not startthe bundle has no PLAY.BAT, or the entry point is wrongopen the .zip and check that PLAY.BAT runs the game's executable
Nothing happens when you tap Playthe emulator is restarting to boot the gamewait a few seconds — the process restarts on purpose, and the game starts in the new process
The keyboard is in the waythe pads take the sides of the screenturn the phone to portrait for a single strip along the bottom
The mouse does not clickthe game wants the mouse helddrag for movement; the on-screen RMB key, or a second finger on the picture, is the right button
You cannot pick the Download folderAndroid will not grant an app the whole Download folderpick another folder, such as Documents
You get a notification promptthe app wants to show the "back to the game" notificationallow it or decline it — you can decline, you just lose that notification
The game is slowthe emulator runs the original hardware, not a fast oneit is what the 1994 machine did. It is not the phone being slow.

Where to go next​

The releases page carries the APK and its checksum.

Thank you, DOSBox​

None of this is possible without the DOSBox project.

DOSBox was written by the DOSBox Team — a volunteer crew who took an operating system from 1994 and made it runnable on every machine the world has produced since, including the ones that did not exist yet. They kept it alive for three decades and gave it away to anyone who wanted it. WhereQ-DOSBox is a thin layer on top of their work. The originality, the craft and the generosity are entirely theirs.

This port is built on Em-DOSBox, the Emscripten build of DOSBox r4305 by Boris Gjenero, which grew out of Ismail Khatib's first attempt to compile DOSBox with Emscripten, and rode on the Emscripten work of Alon Zakai. The SDL2 library by Sam Lantinga carries the window, the audio and the touch input.

DOSBox is a trademark of the DOSBox project; this port is not affiliated with or endorsed by the DOSBox team.
